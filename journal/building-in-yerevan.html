Building in Yerevan: What Armenian tuff stone teaches you about warmth

I came to Yerevan for the first time in the middle of January. The temperature was -12°C and the city was under a thin skin of snow. What I remember most from that first walk through Kentron is not the cold, but the warmth — the way the old buildings glowed even in the flat January light, a deep rose-pink that made the streets feel inhabited and alive even with no one on them. The buildings are made of tuff: a volcanic stone quarried from the hillsides south of the city, the same material Yerevan has been built from for at least five hundred years.

Working in Yerevan changes how you think about buildings and time. A city that was built predominantly in one material, and rebuilt in that same material after each of its historical disruptions, has a visual and tactile coherence that is very rare. The tuff is everywhere — in the Soviet-era apartment blocks, in the Tsarist commercial buildings on Abovyan Street, in the Republican Square ensemble, in the oldest surviving churches of the Geghard monastery. It is the connective tissue of the city's entire visible history.

What tuff actually is

Tuff (tufa in Italian, tufa in Armenian) is a type of rock formed from consolidated volcanic ash and debris. Armenian tuff is principally sourced from quarries around Artashat and Erevan and ranges in colour from pale grey and cream through pink and salmon to a deep wine-red, depending on the iron content and the specific quarry seam. The rose-pink tuff used for most of Yerevan's 19th and 20th century buildings comes primarily from quarries near Artik.

The key physical characteristic that makes tuff so useful is its workability: it is soft enough when freshly quarried to be cut with basic hand tools, while hardening significantly after prolonged exposure to air. Traditional Armenian masons could cut complex profiles and decorative elements by hand that would require diamond-tipped machinery in any harder stone. The old Soviet-era apartment blocks in Kentron were built with tuff blocks cut to precision tolerances entirely by hand.

"Tuff holds heat like a living thing. In the morning it is cool. By four o'clock the south-facing walls are warm to the touch — not hot, warm, like a body."

The thermal mass argument

Tuff is a good insulator by stone standards — its porosity means it has less thermal mass than granite or marble — but it is still stone, and it still behaves like stone: slow to heat up, slow to cool down. A tuff wall 30–40 cm thick, which was standard in pre-Soviet Yerevan domestic construction, buffers the temperature swings of a continental climate in ways that modern lightweight construction simply cannot match. The internal temperature in an old tuff building stays comfortable for several hours after external temperature changes. Modern buildings in Yerevan need air conditioning that old tuff buildings never did.

For the Loft Kentron project — a conversion of a 1930s tuff building in central Yerevan — we made the decision early not to cover the tuff walls. They were lime-washed with a thin, breathable finish that cleans the surface without sealing it, and left to breathe. The thermal performance of the existing envelope was retained; the new heating system is sized for a well-insulated building, not a cold, leaky one.

Working with tuff today

Contemporary tuff masonry in Yerevan is still alive — there are fabricators who cut and lay tuff to the same standard as their Soviet predecessors — but it is increasingly expensive relative to the imported materials that dominate new construction. Architects who specify tuff are swimming against a current that runs strongly toward render and tile.

The Gallery of Light competition entry in Yerevan proposed an exterior cladding of large-format, machine-cut tuff panels on a steel frame — a contemporary interpretation of the material that maintained the visual language of the city without pretending to be traditional construction. The key move was the joint detail: we specified an open, recessed joint 18 mm wide between panels, so the panels read as individual elements with clear depth, rather than an imitation of coursed masonry. The result is clearly contemporary while the material places the building firmly in the city's visual tradition.

What it teaches you

Every material has a lesson if you work with it long enough. What tuff has taught me is something about patience and permanence — that the most enduring buildings are those that settle into their place, that take on the colour and texture of their surroundings, that age into beauty rather than away from it. Tuff darkens and deepens with time; the quarried-fresh stone and the century-old wall are both beautiful, but differently so.

Working in Yerevan means working under the pressure of that long history. Every new building sits next to something that will outlast it by a century at minimum. The tuff buildings don't lecture you — they just exist, warm and patient — but they do ask a question that I try to answer with every project: will this thing get better with time, or worse?

The Persian Courtyard: An ancient typology for the contemporary city

The courtyard house is one of the oldest building types in the world. From the atriums of Pompeii to the riads of Marrakesh, the walled courtyard with a planting or pool at its centre has been the dominant residential form across the Middle East, North Africa and the Mediterranean for at least four thousand years. In Iran, the tradition is unbroken from pre-Islamic palatial architecture to the finest Qajar mansions of the nineteenth century. And then, in barely two generations, it almost disappeared.

What replaced it — the perimeter apartment block, the suburban villa, the apartment tower — is in many ways a worse fit for the climate, the culture and the way Iranian families actually want to live. The courtyard house encoded solutions to problems that are still very much with us: extreme summer heat, a culture of privacy, the desire for outdoor space that isn't a balcony overlooking a street. The Courtyard House project in Mashhad was, for us, a chance to think through what those solutions look like translated into contemporary construction and a contemporary family's life.

The howz

The shallow reflecting pool — the howz — is the climatic heart of the traditional courtyard. Evaporative cooling from a body of water is a sophisticated piece of passive engineering: the water surface cools the air above it, and in a walled courtyard that cool air sinks, while warm air rises and escapes. In Mashhad, where summer temperatures regularly exceed 38°C, even a modest howz of three or four square metres makes the outdoor courtyard useable for two or three more hours in the afternoon.

In the Courtyard House we located the howz centrally, at the intersection of the two main axes, and sized it at 4×2 metres. The depth is deliberately shallow — 40 cm — so the reflection is precise and still. Children and adults have sat beside it for an entire evening on hot days. The stone selected for the coping is the same cream limestone used inside, so the boundary between indoors and outdoors reads as a continuous surface interrupted only by the glass threshold.

"Every great courtyard is really an argument about how the family wants to be together: visible to each other across the water, but each room its own world."

The iwan and the threshold

The iwan — the vaulted, open-fronted room that faces the courtyard — is the transitional space that makes the courtyard house feel generous even on its smallest footprint. It is neither fully outside nor fully inside; it is the living room that happens to have no fourth wall.

We reinterpreted the iwan on the south-facing side of the Mashhad courtyard as a 3.8-metre-deep covered porch with a concrete slab ceiling, open to the courtyard on three sides. The structural depth keeps it in shade from late morning to sunset. The family uses it for breakfast in spring and dinner in autumn — the meal times that most benefit from filtered daylight and shade.

Privacy and the street

The Persian courtyard house turns its back to the street — literally. The street façade is typically blank or nearly so: a solid masonry wall with a single gateway, perhaps a pair of windows set high. All the life, light and generosity faces inward. This is often misread by outside observers as a sign of a closed culture. It is actually a deeply sophisticated response to density: everyone in the neighbourhood gets the same experience of walking down a quiet, uncluttered street, while inside each house there is a private garden, sky and water.

Contemporary building codes, setback requirements and homeowners' expectations make a fully inward-facing street wall difficult to achieve. But the principle can be honoured in part: a solid ground floor with high, opaque windows facing the street, all primary glazing facing the courtyard, and a strong planted boundary at the street edge. The house reads as quiet from outside and generous from within. That asymmetry — where the interior experience is entirely different from the street expectation — is one of the pleasures of the type.

What the courtyard house teaches about daylight

A single-loaded corridor, with all rooms facing the courtyard on one side, produces a simple lighting condition: every room has a south, east or west exposure (depending on position) and a view of sky and greenery. There are no interior rooms, no borrowed light, no dark corridors. The hierarchies of space — primary rooms larger, servant spaces smaller — are encoded in the plan as naturally as they are in any basilica.

In Iran specifically, the east-facing rooms catch the cool morning light; the west-facing rooms receive the long afternoon and evening sun and become the primary living spaces in spring and autumn. The north rooms stay cool and are traditionally used as summer sleeping rooms. These orientations are not conventions — they are climatic logic encoded over millennia, and they still work perfectly today.

Is it possible in a city?

The objection most often raised against the courtyard house is density — that it requires too much land to be viable in a city. The history argues otherwise. Traditional Iranian cities were built almost exclusively of courtyard houses on relatively small plots; Tehran's older districts still show the traces of this in their street layouts and plot widths. The issue is not density but plot shape: the courtyard house works best on a roughly square or slightly rectangular plot, deep enough to allow a court of at least 6×6 metres.

On smaller plots — the kind common in contemporary Iranian cities — the courtyard shrinks and must work harder. In those conditions, water and planting become more important, not less: a 3×3-metre court with a well-designed howz, a fig tree and white-plastered walls is still a very good room. The type is scalable in ways that the apartment block or villa simply are not.

We believe the courtyard house deserves a second generation of serious design attention — not as revival or pastiche, but as a living solution to a contemporary problem. The Courtyard House in Mashhad is our attempt to prove the argument.
